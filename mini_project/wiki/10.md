## 2. Foundations: Abstraction Levels & Inquiry

### 2.1 Three Levels of Abstraction (p. 6)

| Level         | Description                                                                              |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Rationale** | Logical basis that makes actions meaningful; supports reasoning about strategy & tactics |
| **Strategy**  | Overall plan addressing scope, context, constraints, and future growth                   |
| **Tactics**   | Concrete actions aligned with strategy to achieve specific ends                          |

> *“Do the difficult things while they are easy and the great things while they are small.”*  
> — Lao Tzu (p. 5)

***

### 2.2 Observation Is Theory‑Laden (p. 7)

Inspired by **Norwood Russell Hanson (1924–1967)**:

*   Observation is never neutral
*   What we perceive is filtered through theory and preconceptions
*   Sensing and interpreting cannot be separated
*   Abstract and concrete perspectives are **complementary**, not opposing

This provides the epistemological basis for moving between abstraction levels in design.

***

## 3. Problem & Solution Formulation

### 3.1 Philosophical Foundation (p. 8)

Based on **Deweyan pragmatism**, using concepts such as:

*   Inquiry
*   Situation
*   Transaction

Key ideas:

*   Problem formulation can begin from **problem → solution** or **solution → problem**
*   Balance between:
    *   Concrete ↔ abstract
    *   Problem-side ↔ solution-side
*   Concrete formulations enable testing
*   Abstract formulations enable generalization and reuse

***

### 3.2 Linked Problem–Solution Inquiry (p. 9)

Design inquiry involves movement between:

*   Concrete and abstract
*   Problem and solution domains
*   Present situations and future options

***

### 3.3 Actual, Mental & Formal Views (p. 10)

Inspired by **Christopher Alexander – *Notes on the Synthesis of Form* (1964)**

| View       | Focus                                          |
| ---------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| **Actual** | Real-world context and real artifacts          |
| **Mental** | Designer’s internal conceptual models          |
| **Formal** | Explicit models, representations, and patterns |

***

### 3.4 Designer Roles (p. 11)

| Type                | Characteristics                                         |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Unselfconscious** | Reacts directly to misfits in reality                   |
| **Selfconscious**   | Uses mental abstraction and intuition                   |
| **Qualified**       | Uses formalized patterns and evaluation of alternatives |

***

## 4. Manifestations & Capabilities as Complementary Concepts

### 4.1 Abstract vs. Concrete (p. 12)

Key relationships:

*   **Manifestations** → how problems appear in reality
*   **Capabilities** → what the solution can do to address them

Both exist at:

*   Concrete level
*   Abstract level

> *Inquiry is movement between situation, problem, and solution.*

***

## 5. Manifestations

### 5.1 Definition & Role (p. 13)

> *“Solving a problem simply means representing it so as to make the solution transparent.”*  
> — Herbert Simon

Manifestations:

*   Objects, actions, or events
*   Represent how a problem appears in the **outer environment**

***

### 5.2 Four Core Activities (p. 14)

| Activity        | Purpose                            |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| **Perceive**    | Understand problem & context       |
| **Form**        | Design constructive solution parts |
| **Consolidate** | Integrate into a coherent whole    |
| **Learn**       | Evaluate and reflect               |

***

### 5.3 The 12 Building Blocks — Perception Terminology (p. 15)

**Problem-Side Concepts:**

*   Problem
*   Outer Environment
*   Manifestations

**Solution-Side Concepts:**

*   Capabilities
*   Inner Environment
*   Leverage Points

**Evaluation & Direction:**

*   Solution
*   Evolvability
*   Merit
*   Mission
*   Potential
*   Horizon

These map directly to **Problem–Solution Canvas** cells.

***

### 5.4 Demand‑Pull (Problem → Solution) (p. 16)

**Pull strategy:**

*   Start with a problematic situation
*   Identify manifestations
*   Develop capabilities iteratively

Example manifestations:

*   Missing information
*   Reduced flow capacity
*   Delayed response
*   Local density buildup

Capabilities emerge as responses (e.g. data acquisition, anomaly detection).

***

## 6. Capabilities

### 6.1 Definition (p. 17)

> *“Organic order is achieved when there is a balance between the needs of the parts and the whole.”*  
> — Christopher Alexander

Capabilities:

*   Features intentionally designed
*   Provided by the **inner environment**
*   Address manifestations in the outer environment

***

### 6.2 Form Activity Revisited (p. 18)

Form focuses on:

*   Constructive elements
*   Structure
*   Architectural coherence

***

### 6.3 The 12 Building Blocks — Form Terminology (p. 19)

Refined terminology on the solution side:

*   Capabilities
*   Inner Environment
*   Leverage Points
*   Solution qualifications
*   Value
*   Mission
*   Properties
*   Horizon

***

### 6.4 Technology‑Push (Solution → Problem) (p. 20)

**Push strategy:**

*   Start with a technological possibility
*   Investigate what problems it could address
*   Explore conditions and stakeholders

Example questions:

*   Can this improve flow?
*   Can it prevent buildup?
*   Can it enhance decision-making?

## 9. Key Takeaways

*   Effective design moves **continuously** between abstract and concrete
*   Manifestations and capabilities are duals
*   Push and pull are complementary, not exclusive

    *   Transferability
    *   Strategic flexibility
