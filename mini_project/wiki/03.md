## Chapter 5: Rationale – What We Devise

### Definition of *Devise* (p.5)

> *What we plan or invent through careful thought, what combinations or applications of ideas we form in our minds, and what we intend to obtain or bring about over time.*

Rationale concerns **intentional design thinking over time**, not just isolated solutions.

---

### Three Levels of Abstraction (p.6)

1. **Rationale**  
   The logical basis that makes actions meaningful and supports reasoning about strategy and tactics.

2. **Strategy**  
   The master plan for addressing the overall problem, including scope, architecture, constraints, and limitations.

3. **Tactics**  
   Concrete actions planned in accordance with strategy to achieve specific ends.

These levels link *why*, *how*, and *what* we do in innovation projects.

## Deweyan Pragmatism (pp.7–10)

### Core Ideas

- Problems do not appear fully formed; they emerge through **progressive inquiry** (p.7).
- Learning happens through **experience and trial & error** (p.8).
- Thinking is triggered by encountering a **problematic situation** (p.9).

> “We only think when we are confronted with a problem.” — attributed to John Dewey

---

### Knowledge in Pragmatic Problem-Solving (p.10)

Problem-solving combines two kinds of knowledge:

- **Warranted assertions**  
  Claims backed by evidence.
- **Apprehensions**  
  Ideas we grasp because they make sense, even if not yet proven.

Apprehensions are often treated *as if* they were warranted, introducing bias.

---

## Values, Perception, and the Appreciative System

### Selective Perception (p.11)

- Facts are relevant only in relation to judgments of value
- Judgments of value are meaningful only in relation to facts
- Reality is selected and structured by interests and standards

> Values and perceptions are inseparable; bias must be handled consciously.

**Reference:**  
Vickers, G. (1968). *Science and the Appreciative System*. *Human Relations*, 21(2), 99–119.

---

### Appreciative Systems (p.12)

- Built through experience, learning, and education
- Help us recognize and act
- Can also make us blind to unfamiliar phenomena

---

## The Four Core Activities (p.14)

1. **Perceive** – understand the problem and its context  
2. **Form** – design constructive solution elements  
3. **Consolidate** – integrate contributions into a coherent whole  
4. **Learn** – appraise and reflect on the design  

These activities structure the Essence approach to innovation.

## Chapter 6: Situation and Problem

### Situation (p.15)

A *situation* is not a single object or event, but a **contextual whole** consisting of:
- Objects
- Events
- Experience
- Judgment (appreciation)

> We never experience objects or events in isolation.

**Reference:**  
Dewey, J. (1938). *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry*, p.66.

---

### Indeterminate Situation (p.16)

Characteristics:
- Disturbed, troubled, ambiguous, confused
- Inherently doubtful
- Cannot be resolved by changing personal mindset alone

The doubt lies in the **situation itself**, not just in the observer.

---

### Problematic Situation (p.17)

- An indeterminate situation becomes *problematic* through inquiry
- Exists prior to cognition
- Recognizing that inquiry is needed is the **initial step** of problem formulation

---

### Determinate Situation (p.18)

- The outcome of inquiry
- Uncertainty is resolved
- The situation becomes unified and closed

> Inquiry transforms an indeterminate situation into a determinate one.

---

## What Is a Problem? (p.20)

> *A problem represents the partial transformation by inquiry of a problematic situation into a determinate situation.*

Key aspects:

- **Contextual**  
  Problems are always situated in a world of objects and events.
- **Partial & evolving**  
  Problems are provisional and may be replaced as understanding deepens.
- **Instrumental**  
  Problems guide inquiry; they are tools for thinking, not static descriptions.

---

## Existential vs. Ideational Subject Matter (p.26)

- **Existential subject matter**  
  Components, objects, events, builds  
  → Can interact with and modify reality

- **Ideational subject matter**  
  Plans, criteria, procedures, ideas  
  → Explain situations and suggest actions

Scientific discoveries (e.g. Higgs Boson, gravitational waves) exemplify ideational constructs becoming existential.

## Perception Terminology: The 12 Building Blocks (p.27)

### Core PSC Elements

- **Problem** – the situation to be handled  
- **Outer Environment** – the domain where the problem exists  
- **Manifestations** – observable signs of the problem  
- **Capabilities** – features designed to address manifestations  
- **Inner Environment** – what provides the capabilities  
- **Leverage Points** – where small changes yield large effects  

### Outcome & Evaluation Concepts

- **Solution** – why the problem is resolved  
- **Evolvability** – capacity to adapt or extend  
- **Merit** – value offered and acceptable limitations  
- **Mission** – tactical objective  
- **Potential** – strategic objective  
- **Horizon** – future trajectory and alignment  

These concepts form the backbone of the **Problem–Solution Canvas**.

## Problem Scenarios (pp.29–33)

### Motivation (p.29)

> *For every complex problem there is an answer that is clear, simple, and wrong.*  
— H. L. Mencken

---

### Context Types – Cynefin Framework (p.30)

Adapted from Snowden & Boone (2007):

- **Simple** – known knowns (sense–categorize–respond)
- **Complicated** – known unknowns (sense–analyze–respond)
- **Complex** – unknown unknowns (probe–sense–respond)
- **Chaotic** – unknowables (act–sense–respond)

> Essence is designed for **complicated, complex, and chaotic** contexts.

---

### Problem Scenario Axes (pp.31–32)

Each problem scenario uses:
- One **vertical axis**
- One **horizontal axis**
- Both are dichotomous

#### Vertical Axis (choose one)

- **Who** – which stakeholders are affected?
- **Why** – purpose (market, safety, health, law, ecology, etc.)

#### Horizontal Axis (choose one)

- **What** – context type
- **Where** – location
- **When** – timing
- **How** – mode of impact
- **How much** – scope or magnitude (risk, cost, loss)

Axes are inspired by the **W5H2** questioning approach.

---

### ClusterDetect Problem Scenarios Example (p.33)

Four quadrants illustrate alternative interpretations, e.g.:

- Site-specific vs. general
- Planning & support vs. analysis & optimization

Each quadrant yields a **distinct problem formulation**, encouraging exploration before committing to one.
