## Core Framing

### Design Cycles

The lecture repeatedly emphasizes a cyclic understanding of innovation and design:

> **Perceive → Form → Consolidate → Learn**

This aligns with iterative, learning-driven innovation rather than linear execution (pp. 1–3).

***

## Digital Innovation (DI)

### Definition

> **Digital Innovation** is the appropriation of digital technologies *in the process of* and *as the result of* innovation.  
> It emphasizes **combinations of digital and physical components** to produce novel products and services. (p. 7)

### Characteristics

*   Sociotechnical in nature
*   Leads to:
    *   Disruption of established practices
    *   New organizational forms
    *   Societal change
*   Requires integration across disciplines (IT, management, business, design)

***

## Design Science Research (DSR)

### Definition

Design Science Research focuses on:

*   Creating and evaluating **IT artifacts**
*   Solving **organizational and societal problems**
*   Producing **new, transferable knowledge**
*   Contributing beyond the local case (p. 7)

### Artifact Types

Typical DSR artifacts include:

*   Constructs
*   Models
*   Methods
*   Instantiations

## Motivation for the Hevner & Gregor Paper

The paper was written to address the observation that:

*   Digital innovation is inherently **design-oriented**
*   Traditional innovation theory insufficiently explains **how digital artifacts emerge**
*   Existing innovation models fail to integrate:
    *   Entrepreneurship
    *   DSR
    *   Strategic innovation practices

**Contribution:** A **matrix-based model** for understanding innovation strategies grounded in DSR (pp. 5–6).

## Knowledge Innovation Maturity Dimensions

Innovation is framed along two orthogonal dimensions (p. 12):

### 1. Application Domain Maturity (Problem Space)

Concerns:

*   Market maturity
*   Problem understanding
*   User needs
*   Industry context
*   Opportunity recognition

### 2. Knowledge Maturity (Solution Space)

Concerns:

*   Existing knowledge
*   Skills and expertise
*   Technological know-how
*   Learning and discovery
*   Novel insights

## Knowledge Innovation Matrix (KIM)

The **Knowledge Innovation Matrix** positions innovation strategies based on the maturity of problems and solutions (pp. 12–13).

### The Four Innovation Strategies

#### 1. Invention

*   **Low problem maturity**
*   **Low knowledge maturity**
*   “New to the world”
*   Rare but high impact
*   Example: Internet, GPS

#### 2. Advancement

*   **High problem maturity**
*   **Low knowledge maturity**
*   Known problem, new solution
*   Often technology-driven
*   Common in DSR projects

#### 3. Exaptation

*   **Low problem maturity**
*   **High knowledge maturity**
*   Existing solutions applied to new problems
*   Borrowed from biology (features evolved for one purpose reused for another)

#### 4. Exploitation

*   **High problem maturity**
*   **High knowledge maturity**
*   Incremental innovation
*   Optimization and refinement
*   Dominates professional software engineering work

> The matrix helps **position projects**, clarify **innovation intent**, and justify **research contribution** (p. 13).

## Effectuation vs. Causation

### Causation

*   Start with a **defined goal**
*   Select **means** to reach that goal
*   Many-to-one mapping
*   Predictive logic

### Effectuation

*   Start with **available means**
*   Explore **multiple possible effects**
*   One-to-many mapping
*   Entrepreneurial logic
*   Embraces uncertainty (pp. 9–10)

### Why Effectuation Matters for Digital Innovation

Effectuation is suitable when:

*   The future is unpredictable
*   Preferences are unclear
*   Markets are endogenous
*   Rules are unstable (p. 11)

This corresponds to **VUCA conditions** and early-stage innovation.

***

## Problem Space for Effectuation

Effectuation operates in environments characterized by:

*   Knightian uncertainty
*   Goal ambiguity
*   Novel markets
*   Unclear evaluation criteria

Notable reference:

*   Sarasvathy, S. D. (2001, 2003)

The “**suicide quadrant**” (new product, new market) is typically avoided by traditional reasoning but embraced by expert entrepreneurs (p. 11).

***

## Innovation as a Process (Not a Structure)

Key orientation of the paper and lecture (p. 15):

*   Focus on **processes**, not organizational charts
*   Focus on **strategies**, not tools
*   Emphasis on **management**, not implementation
*   Innovation and entrepreneurship are integrated
*   Invention is treated as **one type of innovation**, not the whole

***

## Process Model: DSR × Digital Innovation

The integrated model:

*   Connects **DSR cycles** with **innovation strategies**
*   Emphasizes:
    *   Front-end of innovation (idea generation and framing)
    *   Sensemaking over prediction
*   Supports multiple entrepreneurial roles:
    *   Visionary
    *   Technologist
    *   Organizer
    *   Strategist

This process model explains **how different innovation strategies are intentionally selected and executed** over time (pp. 13–14).
